We observe that the $\theta$ that is related by the equation
\begin{equation}
    \sin(\theta) = \frac{\Delta x}{d}
\end{equation}
is equal to the $\theta$ represented by
\begin{equation}
    \tan(\theta) = \frac{y}{D}
\end{equation}
When the distance of both light rays' traveled paths is a multiple of the wavelength of the light, they engage in constructive interference.
We observe the fact that the path difference ($\Delta x$) for constructive waves is
\begin{equation}
    n\lambda
    \label{eq:path difference constructive}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light and $n$ is an integer representing the interested bright fringe. Hence we derive the equation
\begin{align}
    \sin(\theta) &= \frac{\Delta x}{d} \\
    \sin(\theta) &= \frac{n\lambda}{d} \\
    \theta &= \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{n\lambda}{d}\right)
\end{align}
and since we know that the wavelength $\lambda$ of light is orders of magnitudes larger than slit spacing $d$, and hence the value in $\sin$ is close to zero, we can use the sin angle approximation $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \sin(\theta) = \theta$ to simplify to
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \frac{n\lambda}{d}
\end{equation}
relating $\theta$ to $d$ and $\lambda$.
This angle approximation for small values of $\theta$ also apply to $\tan(\theta)$ in the other representation of $\theta$
\begin{equation}
    \tan(\theta) = \frac{y}{D}
\end{equation}
and since we know that this is the same $\theta$ we can simplify to
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \frac{y}{D}
\end{equation}
and putting them together
\begin{align}
    \frac{y}{D} &= \theta \\
    \frac{n \lambda}{d} &= \theta \\
    \frac{y}{D} &= \frac{n \lambda}{d} \\
    y  &= n\frac{D\lambda}{d} \\
\end{align}
to find the distance between fringes we need to find the distance of a fringe to the central maxima and distance of a consecutive fringe to the central maxima and find the the difference between their distances.
\par
We have defined the distance from the central maximum to 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the nth fringe as $y  = n\frac{D\lambda}{d}$
    \item the nth + 1 fringe as $y  = \left(n+1)\right)\frac{D\lambda}{d}$
\end{itemize}
finding the difference $\Delta y = s$ gives us the spacing between adjacent fringes
\begin{align}
    \Delta y = s&= \left(n+1\right) \frac{D \lambda}{d} - n \frac{D \lambda}{d} \\
    s &= \left(n+1-n\right) \frac{D \lambda}{d} \\
    s &= \frac{D \lambda}{d} 
\end{align}
and now we arrive at our destination.

\subsection{Quantitative}

The raw data is too big to fit here. Instead it will be stored in an appendix. Here will be only shown exclusively relevant data, and how it is going to be calculated.

Our raw data consists of different trials, each of which has:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pixels per centimeter-square side length (on the graph marked with a thicker lines)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item It is done 6 times and the mean is used for setting the scale in the software
        \item We chose to measure the side lengths of the squares closer to the interference pattern
    \end{itemize}
    \item Measurement of the milimeter-square side lengths 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Also done 6 times
        \item Could be used to determine uncertainty of our set scale since we are measuring another known size on the image and if it deviates from the expected value it suggests an uncertainty. However this was not used due to the lack of time.
    \end{itemize}
    \item The distance between adjacent fringes for each seperate big fringe
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Each big fringe has been measured from:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item The left edge of the bright fringe to the adjacent left edge of the bright fringe
                \item The right edge of the bright fringe to the adjacent right edge of the bright fringe
                \item Each edge has a 6 measurements done on them
            \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
    \item Average slit distance whose calculation is described below in detail
\end{itemize}
This is shown in figure \ref{img:raw data extract}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \center
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{raw data extract}
    \caption{An extract of the data}
    \label{img:raw data extract}
\end{figure}
Since some images have limited visibility on the edge of each fringe, measurements on such is essentially guesswork and will be ommited for the average fringe spacing calculation.
The fringe distance is calculated by
\begin{equation}
    \text{Average Fringe Spacing} =  \frac{\Sigma\text{all fringe spacing data points}}{\text{number of data points}}
\end{equation}

The distance is calculated by
\begin{equation}
    \text{Distance} = \text{initial distance} - \text{offset} + \text{2 axis translation stage modifier}
\end{equation}
Recall that every distance is based off of the origin point of the measuring tape, which is at 0cm. For instance the table is set to be [initial distance] away from the origin point of the measuring tape where D is one of the values for our independent variables mentioned earlier. However our 2 axis translation stage is closer to the table by a distance [offset] and must have that subtracted from the desired distance. Then we add the 2 axis translation stage modifier because recall that we have two systems responsible for controlling our independent variable, and this one is the one responsible for the "micro adjustments"
