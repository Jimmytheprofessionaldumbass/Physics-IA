The debate of whether light is a particle or a wave was challenged by Thomas Young's famous Double Slit Experiment in Young's Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts of 1807 (\cite{young1807}), where a coherent light source shinning through two tiny slits projected an interference pattern with alternation between light and dark fringes rather than two individual bright fringes in a dark background. Illustrated In Figure \ref{fig:double slit system} (\cite{double slit system}).
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \center

    \caption{double slit system}

\end{figure}
\par
The formula proposed by Thomas Young was
\begin{equation}
    s=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
\end{equation}
\par
In which "S" is the distance between two adjacent bright fringes, $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light, $D$ is the distance between the screen and slits in meters and $d$ is the distance between slits. 
The mathematical geometric derivation is thus justified (\cite{weideman_double_slit}):
Consider a point a on the screen of a double slit system at which two light rays touch from their respective slits.
This is illustrated in figure \ref{fig:double slit diagram}

We observe that the $\theta$ that is related by the equation
\begin{equation}
    \sin(\theta) = \frac{\Delta x}{d}
\end{equation}
is equal to the $\theta$ represented by
\begin{equation}
    \tan(\theta) = \frac{y}{D}
\end{equation}
When the distance of both light rays' traveled paths is a multiple of the wavelength of the light, they engage in constructive interference.
We observe the fact that the path difference ($\Delta x$) for constructive waves is
\begin{equation}
    n\lambda
    \label{eq:path difference constructive}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light and $n$ is an integer representing the nth bright fringe. where $n=0$ is the central maxima and negative values representing the nth bright fringe of the opposite direction. Hence we derive the equation.
\begin{align}
    \sin(\theta) &= \frac{\Delta x}{d} \\
    \sin(\theta) &= \frac{n\lambda}{d} \\
    \theta &= \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{n\lambda}{d}\right)
\end{align}
and since we know that the wavelength $\lambda$ of light is orders of magnitudes larger than slit spacing $d$, and hence the value in $\sin$ is close to zero, we can use the sin angle approximation $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \sin(\theta) = \theta$ to simplify to
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \frac{n\lambda}{d}
\end{equation}
relating $\theta$ to $d$ and $\lambda$.
This angle approximation for small values of $\theta$ also apply to $\tan(\theta)$ in the other representation of $\theta$
\begin{equation}
    \tan(\theta) = \frac{y}{D}
\end{equation}
and since we know that this is the same $\theta$ as the one used in $\sin(\theta)$ which is close to zero, we can simplify to 
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \frac{y}{D}
\end{equation}
and putting them together
\begin{align}
    \frac{y}{D} &= \theta \\
    \frac{n \lambda}{d} &= \theta \\
    \frac{y}{D} &= \frac{n \lambda}{d} \\
    y  &= n\frac{D\lambda}{d} \\
\end{align}
To find the distance between fringes we need to find the distance $y_{a}$ of a fringe to the central maxima and distance $y_{b}$ of a consecutive fringe to the central maxima and find the the difference between their distances $y_{b} - y_{a}$. % Diagram to show

\par
We have defined the distance from the central maximum as 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the nth fringe as $y  = n\frac{D\lambda}{d}$
    \item the nth + 1 (adjacent) fringe as $y  = \left(n+1)\right)\frac{D\lambda}{d}$
\end{itemize}
finding the difference $\Delta y$ gives us the $s$ spacing between adjacent fringes
\begin{align}
    \Delta y = s&= \left(n+1\right) \frac{D \lambda}{d} - n \frac{D \lambda}{d} \\
    s &= \left(n+1-n\right) \frac{D \lambda}{d} \\
    s &= \frac{D \lambda}{d} 
\end{align}
and now we arrive at our destination.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Research Hypothesis and Method variables}

\paragraph{Independent Variable}\leavevmode\\
The independent variable is the distance $D$ between the slit and the screen (See figure \ref{fig:double slit diagram}). There will be two experiments carried out with different setups, namely, the "macro" and "micro" experiments. This is to satisfy the trial quantity and to increase validity
\begin{itemize}
    \item Micro will solely focus on distances within a meter and will include (in cm) 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100.
    \item Macro will have further distances ranging from 3 to 8 meters and will include (in m) 3, 3.5, 4, 5, 5.5, 6, 6.5, 7, and 8.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Dependent Variable}\leavevmode\\
The measured dependent variable is the distance $s$ between two adjacent bright fringes. Illustrated by the figure \ref{fig:S illustration}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \center
    \includesvg[width=0.7\textwidth]{s-image}
    \caption{label of s in the image}
    \label{fig:S illustration}
\end{figure}
\par
And hence our research question is
\par
\begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}{0.8\textwidth}
        \centering
        \textbf{What is the relationship with the distance between screen and slit (from the range of 20cm to 8 m) and the spacing between adjacent bright fringes (in mm) in a double slit system.}
    \end{minipage}
\end{center}
\par
And we hypothesize that the relationship with the distance between screen and slit and the spacing between adjacent bright fringes would be a linear relationship. This is because our mathematically derived equation $s= D\dfrac{\lambda}{d}$ suggests a directly proportional linear relationship between $s$ and $D$.

\subsection{Variable Control and Measurement}
\paragraph{Macro}\leavevmode\\
Macro has two systems for changing the distance, the long range, and the short range. And can be used together to provide a wider range of distances.

The long range consists of the 3m, 5m, 6m, and 8m. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item It will be achieved with the placement of three tables of their respective displacements that the projected screen will hang on
    \item The last 8m is the distance from the origin directly to the wall and thus does not require a seperate table.
    \item The distances are set up relative to the measuring tape origin (0cm)
\end{itemize}

The short range will represent the "micro steps" (0cm, 50cm, and 100cm) 
\begin{itemize}
    \item This will be achieved by placing the laser-slit system on a  \textbf{2 axis translation stage} built with momentum test carts shown in figure \ref{fig:2 axis translation stage}
    \item The distances are set up relative to the momentum test carts' origin rather than the measuring tape origin
\end{itemize}.

By combining two systems we are able to achieve the aforementioned variety of experimental values

Notice how the zero-position of the translation stage is not coincident with the zero position of the measuring tape and is rather closer. This means the true distance $D$ between the slit and the screen is less than the experimental value by a certain offset.

Since some images have limited visibility on the edge of each fringe, measurements on such is essentially guesswork and will be ommited for the average fringe spacing calculation.
The fringe distance is calculated by
\begin{equation}
    \text{Average Fringe Spacing} = \frac{\sum s_i}{N}
\end{equation}
where \(s_i\) is each fringe spacing datapoint and \(N\) is the number of datapoints.

The distance is calculated by
\begin{equation}
    \text{Distance} = \text{initial distance} - \text{offset} + (\text{2 axis translation stage modifier})
\end{equation}
Recall that the distance of tables is based off of the origin point of the measuring tape, which is at 0cm. For instance the table is set to be [initial distance] away from the origin point of the measuring tape where [initial distance] is an experimental value D. However our 2 axis translation stage is closer to the table by a distance [offset] and must have that subtracted from the [initial distance]. Then we add the 2 axis translation stage modifier because recall that we have two systems responsible for controlling our independent variable, and this one is the one responsible for the "micro adjustments".
The predicted values are calculated by plugging the values into the equation we derived in the beginning. Namely:
\begin{equation}
    s = \frac{\lambda d}{D}
\end{equation}
Where lambda is the wavelength of light of our laser (532nm) and d is the distance between slits 0.45mm
The difference is calculated with
\begin{equation}
    \text{Difference} = \left|\text{Measured Value (before sig fig truncation)} - \text{Predicted Value}\right|
\end{equation}
The absolute uncertainty is calculated with max-min:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{[Maximum measured DV]-[Minimum measured DV]}{2}
\end{equation}
